# Decisions log

Every modeling assumption and judgement call in the calculator: what it is and why, and for the Model and Implementation details rows, where to change it.
**D-numbers** are referenced from code comments and from the in-app "How this works" page.
Items marked *(core)* are the central design choices described in [DESIGN.md](DESIGN.md); the rest are
supporting decisions. Dollar figures below describe the data as of the last entry in
"Data update history" at the end; the live values are in `src/data/rules.ts`.

## Model

| # | Decision | Why | Where to change |
|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | *(core)* Year-by-year cash-flow simulation; FIRE numbers are **solved**, not formulas. The 25× figure is shown only as a sanity check. | Social Security timing, two spouses, taxes, and backtesting all need a year-by-year engine. | `src/engine/simulate.ts` |
| D2 | *(core)* Success = never short of money before the **younger** spouse reaches the end age (default 96, Fidelity). Target success rate 90% (the FI Planner's confidence level for "underperforming market conditions"; Fidelity's Planning & Guidance Center measures at the same 90% "in significantly below average market conditions"). | Conservative and matches Fidelity. | Assumptions panel |
| D3 | *(core)* Main method: **block bootstrap** of real history (10,000 paths). Cross-check: every complete **historical window**. Every success rate shown, and every FIRE result, uses the **stricter** (lower) of the two. | Bootstrap makes new but realistic paths; history is real but a small sample. | `src/engine/solve.ts` `combine` |
| D4 | Bootstrap block length **5 years**, circular (wraps from the last data year back to 1871), seeded RNG so identical inputs give identical answers. | Long enough to keep multi-year crashes and inflation spells together, short enough for variety. | Assumptions → chunk size |
| D5 | The solver's search steps use **2,000** of the paths (all of them if fewer; recomputed whenever the market count changes); every answer is then re-checked on all 10,000 and moved later/higher until it passes there. The earliest year is found by bisection for every FIRE type. | Keeps a full recalculation to seconds. Bisection assumes that retiring (for Coast, stopping saving) a year later never does worse. With a mix that changes over time every calendar year still earns the same mix whatever the retirement year (D97), so a later year adds a year of saving; the one thing that differs is how that year's mix shift is taxed (on top of wages in a working year, in the year's tax bill in a retired one), which is small next to a year of saving. The bisection's answer is checked against an exhaustive scan in `tests/solve.test.ts`. | `searchPaths`/`searchPathsFor` in `src/engine/defaults.ts`; `earliestYear`/`minPortfolio` in `src/engine/solve.ts` |
| D6 | Everything is in **today's (real) dollars**. Returns are converted using each historical year's actual inflation; amounts fixed in nominal dollars (cost basis, Roth principal) shrink with it (D63). | No inflation forecast needed; matches FI Calc/ProjectionLab "real" mode. | — |
| D7 | Money moves at the **start of each year** (contributions, withdrawals), then the year's return applies. | Same as FI Calc. Withdrawals-first is slightly conservative. | `src/engine/simulate.ts` |
| D8 | *(core)* In any given year one asset mix for all invested accounts, rebalanced yearly. "Asset mix over time" (D97) sets each calendar year's mix: by default **Vanguard's Target Retirement glide path** to the year the first person turns 65 (90% stocks early, 50% at 65, 30% from 72; the rest bonds), or Fidelity's fixed **70% stocks / 25% bonds / 5% cash**, or a mix of the household's own. Fund fees **0.10%/yr** subtracted from returns. | A glide path is what most retirement savers hold through a target-date fund; Vanguard's is the largest series (D97). Fidelity's FI Planner mix was the only default until D97 and stays a choice; saved plans keep it (D65). | Inputs panel → Investments |
| D9 | The **cash account** (emergency fund) earns the T-bill return, not the portfolio mix. | It is cash. | `src/engine/simulate.ts` |

## People, work and spending

| # | Decision | Why |
|---|---|---|
| D13 | *(core)* Two-person household. Both spouses stop working in the same calendar year (the "household retirement date"); both are assumed alive through the whole plan. | Keeps the model and results simple; a survivor test was considered and declined (D89), as were separate retirement years per spouse. |
| D14 | Ages are computed by calendar year (`year − birth year`). 59½ access starts the **calendar year a person turns 60**; Medicare (65) starts the calendar year they turn 65. | Annual model; 59½ rounded the conservative way. |
| D15 | Salaries and contributions grow **1.5%/yr above inflation** (Fidelity) until work stops, but 401(k)+IRA and HSA contributions are **capped every year at the IRS limits**, which stay flat in today's dollars (catch-ups from 50 / 55). Money above a limit is not saved anywhere else and does not lower taxed wages (D88). Employer match is not capped. | Limits only keep pace with inflation; letting contributions outgrow them would be optimistic. |
| D16 | While working, the paycheck covers all spending; the portfolio only receives contributions (plus D17 items not in today's budget, and D49), and each account pays the yearly tax on its own dividends and interest (D70). | Simplest faithful model of "still working". |
| D17 | **Dated items before retirement.** An *ongoing* item already running at the plan start (a mortgage, rent received) is part of today's budget: the paycheck covers it until retirement, and it counts from the retirement date on. Every other item dated before retirement (a roof, a car, a new ongoing cost, a home sale) moves money that year: costs come from cash, then the brokerage account (paying federal + state tax on the realized gain, stacked on wages); inflows are saved to the brokerage account at full basis, after income tax on taxed income (D66). A cost that cash and brokerage can't cover **fails the path**. | Costs not in today's budget are real and must be paid from savings; for Coast FIRE they span every year until the coast age. Items already paid today would otherwise be counted twice (the Traditional/Chubby defaults subtract them, D18). Failing when savings can't pay is consistent with "never run out"; with a meaningful brokerage balance it rarely triggers. Also keeps success non-decreasing in the retirement year (a pre-retirement home sale no longer vanishes if you retire later). |
| D18 | Traditional spending must **exclude** healthcare and dated items (added separately). The Fidelity default is **0.85 × (current spending − ongoing dated expenses already being paid today)**. Healthcare paid out of pocket today must be subtracted by hand (the hint says so). | Otherwise a mortgage listed as a dated item would be counted twice. |
| D19 | "Fixed dollars" dated items (e.g. mortgage P&I) shrink by **each path's own inflation** since the plan start. | Exact treatment of nominal payments. |
| D20 | Money coming in (dated income items, surplus Social Security) first covers that year's spending (taxed income also adds to that year's tax, D66); any surplus goes into the taxable account with full cost basis. | Home-sale proceeds etc. |
| D57 | Chubby spending defaults to **1.2 × (current spending − ongoing dated expenses already being paid today)** — the D18 base — via a "Use default" button. Clearing it skips the Chubby result. | Online definitions of Chubby FIRE are dollar bands (~$80k–$200k/yr), not a ratio. As a lifestyle it is comfortable, not frugal, but short of Fat FIRE's no-budget luxury (roughly 1.5×+ today's spending). On v1's example plan, 1.0× landed close to Traditional (0.85×): only 1–2 years later and ~12% more money. 1.2× is a clear step up (3–4 years later, ~25% more) while staying well below Fat. Same base as D18 so a mortgage isn't counted twice. Change `CHUBBY_SPENDING_FACTOR` in `src/engine/defaults.ts`. |

## Healthcare

| # | Decision | Why |
|---|---|---|
| D21 | *(core)* Per person: pre-65 cost applies only in years the household is **not working**; Medicare-phase cost from 65 on. Healthcare grows **1.5%/yr above inflation from today**. No ACA subsidies (full price). | Conservative; subsidies need yearly-income (MAGI) modeling, which was considered and declined. |
| D22 | HSA balances pay healthcare first (tax-free). HSA is also the very last resort for other spending (ordinary tax, +20% penalty — see D41). | Standard HSA rules. |
| D58 | Example healthcare costs per person: **$16,000/yr before 65** and **$7,500/yr from 65** (today's dollars). The "?" help lists the 2026 reference prices. Pre-65 is one flat number, not age-rated. | Pre-65: 2026 US average benchmark silver premium is $7,500/yr at 40 (KFF, $625/mo) and ~$15,900 at 60; under the federal age curve (64 pays 3× a 21-year-old) the average from a mid-50s retirement to 65 is ~$14k, plus ~$2–3k out-of-pocket (2026 limit $10,600). Unsubsidized premiums rose 26% for 2026 as the enhanced tax credits expired. From 65: Part B $2,435 + Medigap G ~$2,650 + Part D ~$400 + ~$1,500–2,000 out-of-pocket, dental and vision. State prices vary widely; IRMAA isn't modeled. Sources: KFF 2026 benchmark premiums and older-adults brief; CMS 2026 Medicare costs. Refresh with the yearly data update. |

## Social Security

| # | Decision | Why |
|---|---|---|
| D23 | *(core)* Benefit computed from a pasted SSA earnings record (taxed Social Security earnings), with the statement's full-retirement-age benefit as a fallback. | SSA statements assume you keep working until you claim, which overstates benefits for early retirees. |
| D24 | Past earnings indexed to the latest **average wage index** year; later earnings (past and projected) at face value in today's dollars; the matching **bend points** (first eligibility = AWI year + 2); future earnings capped at the **taxable maximum**. Result is in today's dollars. | SSA's own "today's dollars" method. By default ignores real growth of the national wage index between now and 60, which *understates* benefits (conservative): at 0.9%/yr (1985–2024) by about 22% for someone now 40 and 11% at 50. D77 makes the growth an editable setting. |
| D25 | Claim ages are whole years 62–70. First benefit year counts the months actually received that year: entitlement starts in the birthday month (the month after it at 62), and each month's benefit arrives the following month, so the first year pays 12 − birth month (one fewer at 62), and later years 12; a December birthday claiming at 62 is first paid in February of the next year (11 months that year). Early/late adjustments and spousal benefit (50% of the other's PIA, reduced if started early, no delayed credits) follow SSA rules; spousal benefit starts only once the other spouse has claimed. No earnings test. | SSA rules; the earnings test mostly defers rather than loses benefits. The model is a yearly cash flow, so the first year counts cash received (v1 counted one to two months more, overpaying $2–5k once). |
| D26 | *(core)* Trust-fund cut: 100% before **2032**, then a straight line from **78% (2032)** to **62% (2100)**, flat after (2026 Trustees Report, OASI). Editable, including 100% (no cut) or 0% (no Social Security). | Plans conservatively for the officially projected shortfall. |

## Accounts, withdrawals and taxes

| # | Decision | Why |
|---|---|---|
| D27 | *(core)* Before 59½: cash → taxable → Roth contributions and conversions ≥5 years old → pre-tax with 10% penalty (flagged, not failure, D68) → unseasoned conversions (10% penalty) → Roth earnings (tax + penalty) → HSA. | Keeps penalties as a last resort. |
| D28 | After 59½: RMDs and the bracket fill first, then cash → taxable → more pre-tax → Roth → HSA. In mixed-age households each person follows their own access rules, pre-tax before Roth across the household. | Tax-efficient order: Roth keeps growing tax-free and pre-tax shrinks before RMDs. |
| D29 | *(core)* **Bracket fill** (Roth ladder): every retired year, pre-tax money is withdrawn up to the top of the chosen bracket (default **10%**). What spending doesn't need is converted to Roth. Not done in years the household still works. The year-by-year table shows the Roth money available without tax or penalty each year. | Converting builds early access to pre-tax money only if a conversion seasons (5 years) before its owner's 59½ (the calendar year they turn 60, D14), and pre-tax money comes from the older spouse first (D30); after 59½ the same rule shrinks future RMDs. The maintainer moved the default from 12% to 10%: on the example plan at its 2039 date, 10% needed $2.622M with a 19.0% penalty rate and $365k left in a bad market, vs $2.695M / 37.5% / $74k at 12% and $2.631M / 15.7% / $291k with no conversions. The app doesn't count some benefits of converting (survivor single-filer taxes, Roth worth more to heirs; the survivor test was declined, D89). Plans saved with the old default keep 12% ("How this works" marks it as yours). |
| D30 | Among spouses, pre-tax withdrawals and fill come from the **older** spouse first. | Their account reaches 59½ and RMD age first. |
| D31 | RMDs from age **75** (born 1960+) or **73** (born 1951–59) using the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table — also in years still working (D49). | SECURE 2.0. |
| D32 | Federal tax: current-year MFJ brackets, standard deduction + extra per spouse 65+, LTCG 0/15/20% stacked on ordinary income, Social Security taxation (provisional income), 3.8% NIIT. Indexed amounts stay constant in real terms; **non-indexed** thresholds ($32k/$44k Social Security, $250k NIIT) shrink with the price level. The temporary 2025–2028 senior bonus deduction is ignored. | Conservative, current law. |
| D33 | State tax: one flat rate (default **5%**) on taxable income excluding Social Security. | State rules vary widely; a single editable rate covers any state, including 0% for no-income-tax states. |
| D34 | Brokerage dividends and interest, and cash-account interest, are **taxed every year** (D70); the rest of the growth is taxed as a gain when sold. | v1 left them untaxed on the grounds that most dividends fall in the 0% gains band. That didn't hold: the bracket fill and wages push investment income out of the band, and interest never qualifies for it. |
